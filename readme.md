## 1 Facial reconstruction

**Definition.** The amalgamation of artistry with forensic science, osteology, osteometry and anthropology to recreate the face of an individual from skeletal remains.

It is of two types: 2D and 3D reconstruction.

### 1.1 2D reconstruction

- Sketching of facial features, either by hand or by using software.
- Economical, fast and efficient.
- Majorly affected by the skill of the artist, the quality of the original photograph and the completeness of the skull.

#### Method

- Developed by Karen T. Taylor in the 1930s.
- Tissue depth markers are adhered at anthropological landmarks on the unidentified skull, which is then photographed.
- Lateral photographic plates are the foundation for facial drawings done on transparent vellum.

#### ARFS

Computer Assisted Recovery Enhanced System

#### FACES

Forensic Anthropology Computer Enhancement System

#### About the software

- Capture and digitise radiographs, photographs and skull images to electronically overlay facial features and tissue depths.
- Produce better images than hand drawing and speed up the reconstruction.

### 1.2 3D reconstruction

- Expensive and time consuming.
- The skull sits on a workable stand so it can be tilted and turned in all directions.
- Clay modelling and computer software can both be used.
- With software, scanned photograph features of the unidentified remains and stock photos of other facial reconstructions are manipulated.

**Frankfort Horizontal Position (FHP).** Ideally the skull is held in this position. The anatomical reference line connects the lowest point of the eye socket to the upper margin of the ear canal.

## 2 10 steps of facial reconstruction

- The skull is the most valuable remain.
- It may or may not be covered with soft tissue.
- If there is minimal or no soft tissue covering, the skull is thoroughly examined.

### 2.1 Importance in forensic anthropology

1\. Identification of unknown remains

- Helps estimate the facial appearance of an unidentified person from their skull.
- The reconstructed face can be compared with missing-person photographs to assist identification.

2\. Helps in recognition

- Gives a visual representation of the face that relatives, friends or the public may recognise.
- Recognition can give investigators valuable information, although scientific methods are required for confirmation.

3\. Useful in decomposed or degenerated bodies

- Useful when a body is decomposed, skeletonised, burned or damaged, making facial recognition difficult.
- The skull can be used to reconstruct an approximate face even when the original facial tissues are no longer present.

4\. Helps in generating leads

- Reconstructed images can be circulated through newspapers, social media and police networks to seek information.
- Public recognition may provide leads about the person's identity, family or background.

5\. Use of modern techniques (CT scan, MRI, X-ray)

- CT scans, X-rays and suitable MRI data help examine anatomical structures and support digital facial reconstruction.
- Computer software and 3D modelling improve visualisation and documentation, making reconstruction more efficient and reproducible.

### 2.2 Limitations

1\. Insufficient tissue thickness data

- Some facial bones may be unavailable, so the reconstruction is only partly completed. Example: with a missing mandible, only a partial reconstruction is possible.
- There is limited data on average facial tissue thickness by age and sex.

2\. Lack of methodological standardisation

- A major limitation.
- A single, official method of facial reconstruction is required.

3\. Major setback in eyes, nose and hairstyle

- These are the features most likely to be recalled by a witness.

4\. Subjectivity

- The position and general shape of facial features are determined mainly by the skull.
- Because of artistic subjectivity, the reconstruction reveals only the type of face the person had.

## 3 Superimposition

Facial reconstruction through superimposition.

### 3.1 General

- Comes under the techniques of forensic visual reconstruction.
- A technique by which a recovered skull can be matched with photographs. Very helpful for identification.
- A more recent photograph is better. One lateral-view photograph can be used.

#### Process

1. **1**Prepare two negatives: one of the person, one of the skull.
2. **2**Magnify appropriately for maximum alignment, so interpupillary distance matches exactly.
3. **3**Place the negatives over each other.
4. **4**Compare points, photograph and analyse for similarities and dissimilarities.

#### Points compared

CanthionNasionNasal spineSupra-orbital ridgesAngle of jawTeeth and others.

#### When the original skull is not accessible

- In some circumstances photographs and X-rays may be the only available information, and access to the original specimen may be restricted due to legal matters, inaccessibility, destruction and damage.

**Camera setup.** Ideally the skull is in the Frankfort Horizontal plane. For 35 mm photography, a lens with a focal length of 100 to 200 mm and a camera position of no less than 12 ft (3 m) from the skull will avoid distortion.

#### When the original skull is accessible

- The forensic facial anthropologist should take scaling measurements.
- If possible, tissue depth markers are attached to the skull before the image is produced.
- If not, a scale can be included with some tissue depth markers to indicate overlays superimposed onto images of the skull.

### 3.2 3D skull model

- Used when the original sample is not accessible, directly or via 3D clinical imaging. The 3D model is produced from 2D data such as radiographs and photographs.
- These serve as templates, and multiple views are aligned using cranial points as registration marks.
- Computer modelling software then creates a 3D model, distorting a template mesh to reproduce skull morphology.
- Extrapolation of surface morphology between views is inevitable. The more views available, the more accurate the 3D skull model.

### 3.3 3D skull model via clinical imaging

- Where soft tissues are present, cross-sectional data is created by CT scan, or surface scans can be used, to produce a 3D digital model of the skull.
- With CT data, slice thickness, scan plane, spatial resolution, filters and angle of rotation all affect the resulting 3D model.
- Dental fillings and appliances cause artefacts and may need manual intervention.

#### Software renders CT cross-sections as 3D models in three stages

1. **Segmentation**Isolation of tissue by thresholding.
2. **Interpolation**Between slices, to create a smooth surface.
3. **Illumination**Of the surface.

#### Surface scanners

LasersPhotographic projectionHolographic projection all create 3D models.

### 3.4 Craniofacial superimposition

- Creates a photographic image of the skull that is superimposed on an antemortem photo of the person.
- Using a camera solves some problems of direct cranial reconstruction, such as tissue thickness and the artist's ability.
- It creates new problems, mainly how to photograph the skull in the exact position and at the same distance as the antemortem photo.
- Images comprise wave fronts of reflected or emitted energy captured by a camera system and recorded on film, magnetic media, optical disc and so on.
- The use of CCTV for security has increased the use of video image analysis in support of crime investigation.
- Identification is most often achieved by facial comparison: the image of an offender is compared systematically to that of a suspect.

#### Identification

Occurs when there is no doubt that person A and person B are one and the same. Only achieved with a unique identifying mark, or when similarities are numerous enough to be overwhelming.

#### Recognition

Occurs when similarities are strong enough that someone who knows the person well, or has studied the image in detail, believes the subjects could be one and the same.
